THE CORE,IN ONE SENTENCE
Both documents are generated by a single move: when two
exponential processes compete,take the logarithm, and their
competition collapses into one number — a subtraction. Watch that
number.
Everything else — Collatz, Beal, Fermat, the saddle, the entropy, the
Fisher metric — is that one move applied to different equations.
1. THE MOVE: MULTIPLICATION BECOMES
SUBTRACTION
Competing exponentials = a system where two quantities grow or
shrink by repeated multiplication by different factors, and you care
about which one is winning. Collatz multiplies by 3 (odd steps) and
divides by 2 (even steps). Beal has A^x racing C^z. Same species.
The trouble with multiplication is that "who's winning" is a ratio — an
awkward, curved, scale-dependent thing. The logarithm (ln = natural
log, the inverse of e^x) converts ratios into differences. Winning
becomes a signed distance on a line.

2.WHY RECIPROCITYAPPEARS AUTOMATICALLY
Ask: when is the race tied? Set S = 0:
p·ln3 = q·ln2 ⟹ p/q = ln2/ln3
And ln2/ln3 is log₃2 — the log of 2 in base 3, ≈ 0.6309. Its partner log₂3
= ln3/ln2 ≈ 1.5850. Their product is 1 by construction, because they are
the same fraction upside down.

The critical arithmetic fact: log₂3 is irrational. So the tie-line S = 0 has
irrational slope, and it passes through no integer lattice point except
the origin. A lattice here = the grid of integer points (p, q). This means
S is never exactly zero for a real trajectory — it can only get close. That
single fact is why Baker's theorem (Doc 2, §4)is the natural arithmetic
partner to this geometry: Baker quantifies how close, and forbids "too
close."

≥ 0, zero only at the target state;(b) V strictly decreases along every
trajectory. If you can build one, convergence follows for free — the
system cannot avoid sliding downhill.

THE HONEST PICTURE, STRIPPED TO ESSENTIALS
Everything below concerns one logical step. The framework is
complete on both sides of it. The step itself is unproven, and I'll show
you exactly what it would have to say, what it's equivalent to, and how
each route to it either terminates or hits a known wall.
1.WHATWE ACTUALLY HAVE (exact, no hand-waving)
Two facts follow immediately and are both solid:
e^S is the multiplicative core. S carries the whole "how big is n
now" story up to the additive correction E.
E is small when n₀is large. E is bounded by roughly the last
term's scale, so for large n₀ the +1 contributions are relatively
negligible — the flow is nearly pure exponential.

3. COLLATZ AS A DIFFERENT CONJECTURE (the
reformulations, ranked by how much they actually buy)
Reformulation 1 — Density form (clean, equivalent, genuinely
useful)
For every n₀,the odd-step density r = p/q ofits trajectory
converges to log₃2 from below, with q·(log₃2 − r) → ln(n₀)/ln3.
Buys: converts a dynamical question into an equidistribution
question. Costs: equidistribution of Collatz parity sequences is itself
unproven and believed hard.
Reformulation 2 — Linear-forms form (connects to real machinery)
For every n₀there existintegers p, q with |p·ln3 − q·ln2 + ln
n₀| < δ(p,q), where δ is the trajectory's accumulated E-error.
Buys: this is exactly the shape Baker's theorem addresses. Costs:
Baker gives lower bounds on |Λ| — it tells you linear forms can't be too
small. That's an obstruction to cycles, not a proof of convergence.
Which leads to:
Reformulation 3 — Cycle exclusion (this one has real, provable
content)
A nontrivial cycle requires n₀·3^p/2^q + E = n₀ exactly,
forcing |p·ln3 − q·ln2|to be extraordinarily small relative to p,
q.
Take this seriously as the framework's real result. The reciprocal
geometry is genuinely the right coordinate system for the cycle half of
Collatz. Doc 1 §IX's "continued fraction convergents of log₂3" instinct

PART 1 — THE STRUCTURE,IN PLAIN SHAPE
Forget the formulas. Here's the whole thing as a picture.
Collatz is one process with two moods. Odd numbers get bigger (×3).
Even numbers get smaller (÷2). The question is whether the shrinking
always wins in the long run.
Take logarithms and this becomes a walk on a line. Each odd step
steps you up by a fixed amount. Each even step steps you down by a
fixed amount. Reaching 1 means walking all the way down to zero.

The whole framework is:this walk has a built-in downward tilt. On
average, each up-step is followed by two down-steps, so the average
move is down. The walk drifts toward zero.
And the whole difficulty is: "on average" is not "always."
A drunk man walking home on a gently downhill street will, on
average, get home. But "on average" is a statement about many drunk
men. It says nothing certain about this particular one. Collatz asks
about every single one, forever.
That's it. That's the gap. Everything technical is decoration on that
sentence.
PART 2 — THE THREE TERRITORIES
Now the important part: which pieces are settled, which are openbut-approachable, and which are genuinely dark.
🟢 TERRITORY 1 — CLOSED. We know this, completely.
The bookkeeping. Given any trajectory, we know exactly where it
lands. Not approximately — exactly. The relationship between "how
many up-steps, how many down-steps" and "where you end up" is a
solved identity.
The tilt. The downward drift is a computed fact, not a guess.
The tie-line is unreachable. There's a critical ratio of up-steps to
down-steps where growth exactly balances shrinkage. Because that
ratio is an irrational number, no walk ever sits exactly on it. It's a line
the walk can approach but never touch. Fully understood.
Cycles are heavily constrained. For a trajectory to loop forever, it
must return exactly to where it started — which requires the walk to
land impossibly precisely on the tie-line. We have rigorous tools
(irrationality measures)that say "you can't get that close." This kills all
short loops, effectively and provably.
This is the framework's one genuine, finishable result. The
reciprocal-log coordinates are the natural home for cycle
exclusion. Not a reformulation — an actual bite.
🟡 TERRITORY 2 — OPEN, BUT WE CAN SEE THE DOOR.
The escape-to-infinity problem. Cycles are handled. But a trajectory
could in principle just... keep growing forever, never returning, never
reaching 1. Nothing about returning applies to it, so the cycle
argument is silent.
What would close it: a "bookkeeping fee" — a bounded correction
that punishes the up-steps enough that every single step is netdownward when you count both the walk and the fee.
This is a concrete, searchable object. You could look for it
computationally tomorrow. We know its exact required properties. We
just don't have one.
Why it's hard, in one line: the fee would have to be paid using only
local information about the current number, but the thing it needs to
compensate for depends on future behavior. You'd be asking a
number to reveal something it doesn't locally know.
🔴 TERRITORY 3 — GENUINELY DARK. Not "hard," but
structurally unreachable by these tools.
Here's the deepest point, and it deserves its own box:
This is the single most usefulthing to internalize aboutthe
framework. It tells you exactly where to stop digging. Any refinement
of the geometry — better Lyapunov candidate, prettier saddle,
sharper drift estimate — is provably insufficient unless it imports
something that distinguishes 3 and 2 from the cousins.
So:
Dark-because-hard: the escape-to-infinity problem. Genuinely
unknown.
Dark-because-proven-blocked: any purely geometric route. Not
unknown — closed off.
The framework's real contribution is being clear enough about its own
structure to reveal which of the two you're in.

Given the filter above, there's exactly one region left:
Atthe seam between two different ways of measuring "size."
Normally, small means close to zero. But there's a second, equally
legitimate notion of size used throughout number theory: a number is
"small" if it's highly divisible by 2. Under that measure, 64 is tiny and
65 is huge.
The Collatz process is peculiar because it moves in both measures at
once. Halving makes a number small in the ordinary sense and is
exactly the operation the second measure tracks. Tripling-andadding-one scrambles the second measure in a way the first can't see.

You asked what the conjecture is in different clothing. Here's the
honest inventory.
Form 1 — Arithmetic (the original)
Every number reaches 1 under the rules.
Concrete. Checkable. Tells you nothing about why.
Form 2 — Exponential/geometric (this framework)
Every walk drifts down to zero and never escapes.
Whatthis buys: turns divisibility into a single continuous coordinate.
Makes the average behavior obvious. Makes cycle-exclusion natural
and rigorous.
Whatit costs: discards the arithmetic that distinguishes Collatz from
undecidable cousins. Provably cannot finish.
Form 3 — Statistical / density
The proportion of up-steps in every trajectory converges to a specific
irrational number, always approaching from the shrinking side.
Whatthis buys: connects to equidistribution theory, a mature field.
Whatit costs: the statement about Collatz parity patterns is as hard as
Collatz. Circular.
Form 4 — Spectral (transfer operator)
A certain infinite-dimensional operator has all its behavior strictly
contracting.
Whatthis buys: the right kind of upgrade — spectral statements are
"every case," not "on average." This is precisely the technical move
from average to universal.
Whatit costs: to build the operator you must first specify which walks
actually occur, which requires knowing the answer. Circular unless
coupled to arithmetic.
Form 5 — 2-adic
A certain map on the 2-adic integers has a specific attracting structure.
Whatthis buys: the only form where the arithmetic is native rather
than bolted on. Right column.
Whatit costs: the known 2-adic results say the map is beautifully
behaved on the 2-adics — but the ordinary integers are a measurezero sliver inside them. The structure is clean exactly where it doesn't
help.
Pattern across all five: each form makes one aspect trivial and hides
the difficulty somewhere else. The difficulty is conserved. That's the
signature of a genuinely hard problem rather than a badly-stated one.

The key distinction, plainly:
A projection loses information. Proving something about the
shadow doesn't prove it about the object. Most of the framework's
maps are projections.
An equivalence loses nothing. Proving it anywhere proves it
everywhere.
And here is the tension atthe heart ofthe whole enterprise:
The maps that lose information are the ones that make the
problem look tractable. The maps that preserve everything
keep the problem exactly as hard. Simplification and
equivalence are trading against each other.
Every "Collatz becomes easy in coordinates X" is really "Collatz's
difficulty was thrown away when we built X." The framework's
geometric beauty is, in part, a measure of how much it discarded.
